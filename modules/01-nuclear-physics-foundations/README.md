# 01 · Nuclear physics foundations

*Atoms, nuclei, binding energy, and radioactive decay — the vocabulary everything else is built on.*

## Overview

Before reactors, radiation protection, or fuel cycles, you need a solid picture of the **nucleus**: what it's made of, why it holds together (or doesn't), and what happens when it doesn't. This module builds that picture from the ground up and ends with the tools — decay laws, activity, half-life — that the rest of the field uses daily.

## Prerequisites

- High-school physics (atomic structure, energy, basic forces)
- Comfort with exponentials and logarithms

## Learning objectives

After this module you can:

- Describe the composition of a nucleus using mass number *A*, atomic number *Z*, and neutron number *N*, and use standard isotope notation (e.g. ²³⁵U₉₂)
- Explain why nuclei stay bound and read a binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, identifying where fusion and fission extract energy
- List the main radioactive decay modes (α, β⁻, β⁺, electron capture, γ) and balance decay equations
- Solve problems with the exponential decay law: activity, decay constant, half-life, mean lifetime
- Explain what a decay chain is and why natural radioactivity is all around us

## Topics

### 1. From atoms to nuclei
- Quick history: from Dalton to Rutherford's scattering experiment to the nuclear model
- Nuclear notation, isotopes, isotones, isobars
- Why the neutron changes everything (no Coulomb repulsion → nuclear glue)

### 2. What holds the nucleus together
- The strong nuclear force: short range, charge-independent, saturating
- Mass defect and binding energy: *B = (Zmₚ + Nmₙ − M)* · *c²*
- The semi-empirical mass formula (SEMF) and each term's physical meaning (volume, surface, Coulomb, asymmetry, pairing)
- **Reading the binding-energy curve**: the iron peak, why light nuclei fuse and heavy nuclei fission

### 3. Radioactive decay
- Decay as a random process — why it's probabilistic at the single-nucleus level
- **Alpha decay**: heavy nuclei, tunneling picture (qualitative), recoil
- **Beta decay** (β⁻, β⁺) and electron capture: the role of the weak force, neutrinos, why it changes *Z* but not *A*
- **Gamma emission** and internal conversion: de-excitation after decay
- Decay energy (*Q*-value) and where it goes (kinetic energy, recoil, neutrinos)

### 4. The mathematics of decay
- The decay law: *dN/dt = −λN* → *N(t) = N₀e^(−λt)*
- Activity *A = λN*, the becquerel (1 Bq = 1 decay/s) and the curie (historical unit, 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq)
- Half-life *t½ = ln 2 / λ*, mean lifetime *τ = 1/λ*
- Mixtures of nuclides, secular equilibrium in decay chains
- The natural decay series (uranium, thorium, actinium) and naturally occurring potassium-40 — and why radon is a real public-health topic

## Key terms

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Nuclide | A specific nucleus with given *Z* and *N* |
| Isotope | Nuclides with the same *Z*, different *N* |
| Binding energy | Energy needed to fully separate a nucleus into protons and neutrons |
| *Q*-value | Energy released (or required) by a reaction or decay |
| Activity | Number of decays per unit time (Bq) |
| Half-life | Time for half of a population of nuclei to decay |

## Self-check questions

1. Write the full decay equation for ²³⁸U → ²³⁴Th + α. Verify charge and mass number balance.
2. A source has an activity of 5×10⁹ Bq and a half-life of 8 days. What is its activity after 32 days?
3. Why does the binding energy per nucleon *decrease* for nuclei heavier than iron?
4. Why does beta decay change an element's identity while gamma decay does not?
5. What is the physical meaning of the pairing term in the semi-empirical mass formula?
6. Explain why a fresh nuclear waste stream is initially much more radioactive than natural uranium ore, even though the uranium content is similar.
7. In a decay chain at secular equilibrium, how do the activities of parent and daughter compare — and why?
